ABR Physics & Radiation Biology Qualifying Exam: What to Expect on Test Day

Ready to sit for the Medical Physics for Radiation Oncology and Radiation and Cancer Biology parts of the ABR Qualifying Exam? Here is what to expect on your test day.

These two computer-based remote exams form key portions of the ABR Radiation Oncology Qualifying Exam, distinct from the Clinical Radiation Oncology part and the later oral Certifying Exam. Together they assess your foundational knowledge in medical physics as applied to radiation oncology and the principles of radiation and cancer biology. You can take them together on the same day or schedule them separately. The ABR typically offers both exams annually in June, with results generally posted in myABR within about a month. Review the technical requirements and rules below carefully before your exam date.

System & Technical Requirements

Both exams are administered remotely after you register through myABR. Before test day, complete the Exam Readiness Check and Monitor Performance Self-Check on the exact computer and in the exact location you plan to use for testing.

Basic requirements include:

  • Computer: Desktop or laptop (tablets are not permitted) running Windows 10 or macOS X 10.11 or higher.
  • Memory & storage: A minimum of 4 GB RAM and 10 GB of free disk space.
  • Monitor: 13 inches or larger with 1080p minimum resolution. Only one monitor is allowed, so disable your laptop screen if you plan to test on an external display.
  • Browser: The latest version of Google Chrome or Microsoft Edge.
  • Audio & camera: A working microphone and computer speakers, plus a webcam of at least 640 x 480 resolution at 10 frames per second. The camera is used for proctoring, virtual backgrounds are not permitted, and a side-view setup is required so proctors can see your full testing area.
  • Internet: A reliable broadband connection.
  • Keyboard and mouse.

You are fully responsible for confirming your setup meets these requirements ahead of time. ABR staff cannot provide individual technical support for personal equipment on exam day.

Time, Environment, and Allowed Items

Each part, whether Medical Physics or Radiation and Cancer Biology, takes approximately 2.5 hours and is delivered in a single session. If you are taking both on the same day, they run as two separate sessions with a required 60-minute intermission between them.

Permitted:

  • A private, quiet space free of other people and interruptions.
  • Beverages and snacks.
  • A small dry-erase whiteboard (18 inches or smaller on one side), markers, and an eraser. The board must be wiped clean at both the start and end of each session, and pen or paper is not allowed as a substitute.
  • A cell phone or mobile device, kept visible on your work surface on the same side as the webcam, usable only to contact the ABR Exam Day Help Desk for connectivity issues or during breaks and the intermission.
  • Facial tissue and certain personal medical items, per the full ABR permitted list.
  • The embedded on-screen calculator built into the exam software.

Prohibited:

  • Other individuals in the room.
  • Open books, notes, study materials, or any external digital resources.
  • Personal calculators, scratch paper, pens, or pencils.
  • Headphones, earbuds, or anything in or over the ears, aside from approved medical items such as hearing aids or ABR-provided earplugs where applicable.
  • Additional monitors, cameras, recording devices, or virtual backgrounds.

Breaks & Confirmation

Within each session, you control when and how many breaks you take, though content is locked after a break until you resume. Exceeding your allotted break time reduces the time remaining for the exam itself. If you are taking both exams on the same day, the intermission between sessions is a mandatory 60-minute break, and you cannot start the second session early. Keep your webcam on and your connection active during breaks, though you are permitted to leave the room.

On exam day, you will complete an identification check (photo ID plus a selfie) along with a technical verification before the exam begins. Follow all on-screen prompts and confirmations carefully before starting.

How to Prepare for the Physics & Biology Qualifying Exams

Knowing the exam logistics is only half the battle. Grounding your review in the official ABR study guides and high-yield principles across each domain matters more than last-minute cramming.

Medical Physics for Radiation Oncology

Approximate domain weighting for guidance:

Basic physics11–13%
Radiation measurements9–11%
Treatment planning19–21%
Imaging modalities and applications in radiotherapy16–18%
Brachytherapy12–14%
Advanced treatment planning and special procedures15–17%
Safety, QA, and radiation protection11–13%

Radiation and Cancer Biology

Approximate domain weighting for guidance:

Interaction of radiation with matter1–4%
Molecular and cellular damage and repair13–17%
Cellular response to radiation7–10%
Linear energy transfer (LET) and oxygen effect3–5%
Tumor biology and microenvironment3–5%
Cancer biology19–23%
Radiobiology of normal tissues9–12%
Dose delivery11–15%
Combined modality therapy11–15%
Late effects and radiation protection6–9%

Because treatment planning and imaging carry the heaviest weight on the physics side, and cancer biology carries the heaviest weight on the biology side, a study plan that gives those domains proportionally more time, while still covering every topic, tends to pay off. Build familiarity with core concepts, equations, and their clinical applications rather than memorizing in isolation, and complete the Exam Readiness Check with sample questions ahead of time so the interface itself is not a source of surprise. Official ABR study guides, ASTRO resources, and consistent question practice are all worth building into your routine.

Build familiarity with high-yield physics and radiobiology questions and evidence-based rationales before test day.
